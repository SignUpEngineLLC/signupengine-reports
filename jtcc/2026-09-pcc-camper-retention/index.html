PCC camper analysis · summers 2024, 2025 and 2026 · prepared 4 September 2026

83% of campers never return.

Across three summers, 269 different children attended PCC. Of the 190 who have had a later summer available to them, 158 came once and never came back. Only 32 have ever returned, and six have come all three years.

Read this first: the files count sessions, not children

This changes every headline number, so it is worth being clear about before anything else.

The three CSVs contain 623 rows, but many children appear several times within the same file — appears eight times in the 2026 file alone. Each repeat is one session that child booked, confirmed against the source system; the session label itself was dropped from this export.

So the roster is about half the size the row count implies, and the average camper books about two sessions a summer. It also means every row is a purchase, which makes session count usable as both a loyalty signal and a revenue proxy.

Recruitment has to find roughly 80 new families every year simply to stand still. It has been doing that successfully — head count is broadly stable — which is the strongest evidence in this dataset that the acquisition side is working and the experience side is not.

One genuinely encouraging signal

Retention rose from 13.9% to 21.6% — a 55% relative improvement in one year. On a base this small that is 21 children versus 15, so treat it as a hint rather than a trend, but whatever changed between the 2024 and 2025 seasons is worth identifying before it gets undone.

How this compares to other camps

The American Camp Association asks its members a near-identical question each year, which makes the comparison unusually clean.

ACA's CampCounts survey asks camps what percentage of this summer's campers also attended the previous summer, explicitly instructing them not to remove campers who age out. Day camps reported a mean of 62% and a median of 67%. Across all camp types the mean was 60%.

Families are not returning either

Around a fifth of families carry over each year — barely above the individual-camper rate. If children were simply outgrowing the program, younger siblings would keep those surnames on the roster and the family figure would sit well above the camper figure. It does not. Exactly one surname carried from 2025 to 2026 through a different child rather than the same one.

Whole households are leaving, not just individual children who outgrew the program. That points at the family's experience of the camp rather than at the camper's age.

Does age explain any of it?

Alongside surname continuity, the other obvious structural explanation worth ruling out.

The camp skews older than a typical day camp — the median camper is 14, and two-thirds are 12 to 15. Retention is weak across every age band, but collapses entirely at 16 and above.

What a session is worth

Because every row is a booking, session volume works as a revenue proxy. It tells a slightly different — and more useful — story than head count.

The children who stay are the heavier bookers, so the camp holds more of its session volume than it holds of its roster. Better, but nowhere near enough to change the diagnosis.

The single-session trap

Just over half of every intake books exactly one session, and around nine in ten of those children are never seen again — 5 of 57 from the 2024 intake, 6 of 46 from 2025.

This is the largest single addressable block in the data, it is identifiable at the moment of booking, and it is the one place where an intervention could be designed and tested before next summer.

How names were matched, and the margin for error

You flagged spelling and date-of-birth inconsistencies. Here is exactly what was done about them.

Records were linked into individual children using normalized names (accents stripped, nicknames in brackets or quotes pulled out, case and punctuation ignored) combined with date of birth. Every candidate pair in the whole dataset was scored — 48,516 comparisons — rather than only obvious ones.

Deliberate non-matches

  • Two children share a surname and the exact date of birth 22 August 2011, but have unrelated first names. Treated as twins. If they are in fact one child recorded under two names, that child becomes a three-year camper and the 2025→2026 figure ticks up by one.
  • Two records share a surname and date of birth and were treated as the same child, since one first name is a plausible short form of the other.
  • Twenty-five pairs share a date of birth but nothing else. With around 100 children a year, birthday collisions are expected and these were left separate.

The headline finding does not depend on any of these judgments

As a stress test, retention was recalculated by matching on name alone, ignoring dates of birth entirely — the most generous possible linkage. It produced identical figures: 15 returners in 2025 and 21 in 2026. Even if every borderline call above were wrong in the direction of more matching, retention would move by one or two children out of a hundred.

The realistic margin for error on the retention rates is about ±2 percentage points. That is nowhere near enough to close a forty-point gap against the industry benchmark.

What this proves, and what it does not

Being precise about this matters, because the obvious conclusion is only partly supported.

Well supported by the data

  • Retention is severely below industry norms, at roughly a third of the ACA day camp average, and this is not an artifact of name matching or of children aging out.
  • Acquisition is working. The camp finds around 80 new families a year, reliably, which is why head count looks stable.
  • Whole families leave, not just individual children, which rules out the gentler explanations.
  • Single-session campers are the highest-risk group, they are just over half of every intake, and they are identifiable at the point of booking.
  • Retained campers are worth materially more than acquired ones — roughly 1.5 times the session volume each — so the cost of this retention rate is larger than the head-count gap alone suggests.

Not established by this data

  • Why families leave. Program quality is a reasonable hypothesis, but price, dates clashing with school or travel, transport, staff turnover or a competing local program would all produce this same pattern.
  • Whether booking depth causes loyalty or simply reveals families who were already committed. This matters a great deal for what you do about it: if depth is causal, nudging first-timers into a second session is a lever; if it is only a marker, it identifies who to focus on but changes nothing by itself.
  • Whether the improvement from 13.9% to 21.6% reflects a real change or ordinary variation in small numbers.
  • Whether the drop-off is concentrated in particular sessions or programs. Session counts are in this export but session identities are not, so a camper who booked three sessions is indistinguishable from one who booked three of the same thing.

The cheapest next step is not more analysis

It is talking to the 93 families who left after 2024 and the 76 who left after 2025. A short exit survey of even thirty of them would settle the question of cause faster and more reliably than anything further that can be extracted from these three files.

Questions about the data

Answering these would materially sharpen the analysis.

  1. Session and program IDs are the confirmed next data pull. Which sessions a child chose, and in what order, is the missing view most likely to explain the drop-off — and it is the natural home for a separate analysis rather than an extension of this one.
  2. Do these files cover the entire camp, or a single program or location? A 269-child, teen-skewed population would behave very differently as one specialty program than as a whole camp.
  3. Is the 2026 file final, or was it pulled mid-season? An incomplete 2026 would understate current retention.
  4. Is there a household or parent identifier in the source system? Surname matching is a rough proxy and misses families with different surnames.
  5. Can financial data be joined? Retention matters mainly because of what it costs, and none of these files contain fees paid.
  6. Was there a change to the program, pricing or staff between the 2024 and 2025 seasons that might explain the retention improvement?
  7. Three dates of birth look like entry errors: one child, , recorded as born January 2024, and two children recorded as four years old at a camp whose median age is 14. Worth correcting at source.
